# English geoscience manuscript writing

The manuscript target is English even when the user's instructions and results are in Chinese. Explain editorial choices in the user's preferred language if useful. Reference language does not determine manuscript language.

- Lead with the finding and follow with its evidence or interpretation. Name the formation, samples, or validation setting when they define the scope of the claim.
- Preserve all values, units, sample/well identifiers, equations, figure citations, uncertainty, comparison baselines, and causal strength. Use past tense for performed methods/observations and present tense for supported interpretations or figure descriptions where suitable; do not apply tense mechanically.
- Follow the target journal's conventions when supplied. Use standard geoscience English by default and describe the specific contribution.
- Separate measured values, inverted estimates, model predictions and interpretations. “Suggests/is consistent with” must not become “demonstrates/controls” through translation. A model's feature attribution is not causal geological explanation.
- Verify formal stratigraphic and geographic names against author-confirmed sources. Distinguish lithostratigraphic units from time units, lithofacies from depositional facies, reservoir quality from productivity, shale oil from tight oil, and coalbed methane from coal-derived gas.
- Define NMR, TOC, XRD and model abbreviations once as appropriate. Use consistent British/American spelling per journal, avoid noun piles and unnecessary nominalization, and preserve reference titles in their published language.
- Draft the abstract from actual author findings: unresolved question, approach, quantitative evidence where provided, interpretation and bounded significance. Do not copy wording or reported results from the library exemplars.

When translating, deliver the revised text first. Flag only unresolved terminology or scientific ambiguities that change its meaning.

## Register, cohesion and discipline-specific phrasing

Use the author's clear existing voice where available. Library examples inform rhetorical moves and terminology; do not imitate their errors or recycle their sentences. Use established collocations in context, such as adsorption capacity, pore-size distribution, effective stress, and reservoir quality, checking the specific quantity rather than replacing terms mechanically.

Build topic continuity: introduce the paragraph's claim, develop it with observations and comparison, then connect the qualified inference to the next paragraph. Place familiar information before new information when this improves readability. Keep comparison groups, baselines, pronoun referents, articles and countability explicit. Use active voice to clarify an actor or analytical choice; use passive voice when the sample or procedure is the appropriate focus.

Replace vague praise and stacked hedges with measured differences or a specific source of uncertainty. Use “statistically significant” for “显著” when the statistical evidence supports it. Where the study reports only an association within an interval, write “TOC was positively correlated with U/Th in samples from this interval.” A phrase such as “may potentially suggest” adds no information to this finding.
